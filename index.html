1. Aim

To study the polarisation of light using two polaroids (a polariser and an analyser) and to verify Malus' Law, which states that the intensity of light transmitted through the analyser varies as the square of the cosine of the angle between the transmission axes of the polariser and the analyser.

  • Confirm that unpolarised light is converted to plane-polarised light by a polariser.
  • Measure the transmitted intensity as the analyser is rotated, and verify It = I0 cos2θ.
  • Quantify the agreement between data and theory (linear fit of I vs cos2θ).

3. Theory

Light is a transverse wave — the electric and magnetic vectors oscillate perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Light from ordinary sources (the sun, a candle, a bulb) is unpolarised: the tip of the electric-field vector traces a random pattern in the plane perpendicular to the propagation direction.

Certain materials, called polaroids, have a transmission axis along which the electric field is allowed to pass; the component perpendicular to that axis is absorbed. The light emerging from a polaroid is plane-polarised, with the electric vector confined to a single plane. Passing this plane-polarised beam through a second polaroid (the analyser) reduces its amplitude further, depending on the angle between the two transmission axes.

3.1 Polarisation through two polaroids (Fig. 1)

Fig. 1 — An unpolarised beam is polarised by the first polaroid. The polarised beam of amplitude A0 then falls on the analyser whose transmission axis makes an angle θ with that of the polariser; the transmitted beam has amplitude At.

3.2 Vector geometry of the analyser (Fig. 2)

Fig. 2 — Vector resolution of the incident amplitude A0. The component along the analyser's transmission axis is A0 cosθ (this is transmitted); the perpendicular component A0 sinθ is absorbed.

If A0 is the amplitude falling on the analyser and At is the transmitted amplitude, then from Fig. 2

At = A0 cos θ    — (1)

The intensity of a wave is proportional to the square of its amplitude, so

It = I0 cos2 θ    — (2)

Equation (2) is Malus' Law: the transmitted intensity varies as the square of the cosine of the angle between the two transmission axes.

3.3 Verification strategy

Eq. (2) predicts two equivalent signatures in the data:

  1. A cos2θ curve when It is plotted against θ (peaks at θ = 0°, zero at θ = ±90°, symmetric about 0°).
  2. A straight line through the origin when It is plotted against cos2θ. The slope of this line is the maximum intensity I0 and the intercept is the background/offset (ideally zero).

Both checks are implemented in the Plot section. A linear regression of I vs cos2θ with slope ≈ I0, intercept ≈ 0, and R2 close to 1, is taken as confirmation of Malus' Law.

4. Procedure

1
Set up the optical bench as in Fig. 3: He–Ne laser at 0 cm, polariser at ~50 cm, analyser at ~75 cm, photocell at ~125 cm (distances can be adjusted to suit the bench). Mount the photocell in a transversal saddle for fine alignment.
2
Connect the photocell to the digital multimeter and set the meter to the 2000 μA range. The photocell current is proportional to the light intensity falling on it, so the meter reading acts as a direct measure of It.
3
Switch on the laser. Adjust the heights of the laser, polariser, analyser and photocell so that all four are at the same horizontal level and the beam hits the centre of the photocell.
4
Use the transversal saddle to bring the laser spot to the centre of the photocell, ensuring total illumination of the active area.
5
If the room is not perfectly dark, measure the background current Inoise with the laser switched OFF. Enter this value in the Observations panel — it is subtracted from every reading before the fit.
6
Allow the laser to warm up for ~30 minutes before taking readings. He–Ne tubes drift in intensity for the first half-hour, which would add noise to the data.
7
Set the analyser to the 90° position, then rotate the polariser until the laser light no longer falls on the photocell. This defines the angular reference for the polariser.
8
Take the current reading on the multimeter in steps of 10° as you rotate the analyser from +90° to −90°. Because the meter reading can fluctuate, record both the minimum and maximum value at each angle; the mean of the two is the actual photocell current.

8. Viva-Voce & Short Questions

Q1. What is polarised light? How is it different from ordinary light?
In ordinary (unpolarised) light the electric vector vibrates in all directions perpendicular to the propagation direction, with no preferred orientation. In polarised light the electric vector is confined to a single plane (plane-polarised) or has a well-defined rotational handedness (circularly / elliptically polarised).
Q2. State Malus' Law.
When a beam of plane-polarised light of intensity I0 falls on an analyser whose transmission axis makes an angle θ with the plane of polarisation, the transmitted intensity is It = I0 cos2θ.
Q3. What is the role of the polariser and the analyser?
The polariser converts the unpolarised laser light into plane-polarised light of well-defined orientation. The analyser is a second polaroid whose orientation is varied; the variation of the transmitted intensity with the relative angle is what Malus' Law describes.
Q4. What intensity is expected at θ = 0°? At θ = 90°?
At θ = 0° the two transmission axes are aligned: It = I0 · cos2(0) = I0 (maximum). At θ = 90° the axes are crossed: It = I0 · cos2(90°) = 0 (complete extinction — this is the "crossed polaroids" condition).
Q5. Why is the laser allowed to warm up for ~30 minutes?
He–Ne tubes do not stabilise immediately. The output intensity drifts (and can fluctuate mode-by-mode) during the first half-hour as the gas temperature and the cavity length settle. Taking readings on a cold laser would give a slow systematic drift in the data and would broaden the scatter.
Q6. What is the physical meaning of the ±90° symmetry of the curve?
cos2θ is an even function: cos2(θ) = cos2(−θ). The transmitted intensity depends only on the angle between the two axes, not on which side of the polariser the analyser sits. So the I–θ curve is symmetric about θ = 0°.
Q7. Why is a photocell used as the detector instead of the eye?
The eye is qualitative, slow and biased by dark adaptation. A silicon photocell produces a current proportional to the incident light intensity (over its linear range) and the multimeter reading is objective, instantaneous and quantifiable. The response is also nearly flat across the visible spectrum, so a single calibration works for the He–Ne line.
Q8. What error does the background current Inoise correct for?
If the room is not fully darkened, ambient light contributes a small constant current to the photocell. Subtracting Inoise from every reading removes this offset so that the θ = 90° reading is truly zero (not just "the smallest reading").
Q9. In the linear plot of I vs cos2θ, what should the slope and intercept be?
From I = I0 cos2θ + 0, the slope is I0 (the maximum transmitted intensity) and the intercept is 0. A non-zero intercept usually means uncorrected background light or a non-linear photocell response; a slope that differs from the expected I0 means the laser drifted during the run.
Q10. What would happen if unpolarised light were sent directly into the analyser (no polariser in front)?
Unpolarised light can be thought of as a uniform mixture of all polarisation states. Half of it passes through the analyser regardless of the analyser's orientation, so the transmitted intensity is constant (equal to half the incident intensity) as the analyser is rotated — Malus' Law would not be observed.

9. Precautions

  1. The analyser and the polariser must be at the same horizontal level as the laser beam; otherwise the beam misses one of the apertures or grazes the polaroid at an angle and the reading drifts.
  2. Perform the experiment in a darkened room to minimise background current; measure and subtract Inoise before starting the angular sweep.
  3. The photocell is a sensitive device. Adjust it so that the laser spot falls on the centre of its active area; off-centre illumination gives a non-linear response and reduced current.
  4. Measure the angle between the analyser and the polariser carefully. A 5° error at θ = 60° already produces a ~15 % error in cos2θ, the worst part of the curve.
  5. Do not look directly into the laser beam. Even a low-power He–Ne can damage the retina. Keep the beam path above or below eye level whenever possible.
  6. Allow the laser to warm up for 30 minutes before recording any readings, and avoid touching the polaroid mounts (or breathing on them) during the run — the surface is a soft plastic film.
